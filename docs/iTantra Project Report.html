Abstract

In alert and distress scenarios, transmitting voice is more inclusive than transmitting written text, because it does not require the sender or the receiver to be literate. However, voice is data-intensive. Reproducing intelligible speech requires approximately 256 kbps of raw pulse-code modulated audio, and even the most efficient open-source speech codec available, Opus, has a practical intelligibility floor of about 6 kbps. The narrowband radio links that remain usable when terrestrial infrastructure has failed — LoRa, HF data links and narrowband satellite bursts — provide only a few hundred bits per second. Voice communication over such links is therefore not merely difficult; it is bounded away from possibility, because audio codecs compress the waveform and an intelligible waveform has an irreducible size.

This project presents iTantra, an Android application that resolves this contradiction by removing the waveform from the link entirely. Speech is recognised on the transmitting handset, the resulting text is transmitted as a compact binary frame of a few dozen bytes, and speech is re-synthesised on the receiving handset. Both operators only ever speak and listen; neither reads nor types at any point. The system supports ten Indian languages — Hindi, Gujarati, Marathi, Kannada, Malayalam, Tamil, Telugu, Odia, Bengali and English — and executes entirely on the device, with no cloud service, no SIM, no proprietary software development kit and no network permission declared in the application manifest.

Compression is achieved in three graceful stages. Semantic transcoding converts speech to text, giving a reduction of approximately 770 to 1. Script packing exploits the fact that the frame header already declares the language, allowing each Indic character to be represented in a single byte instead of the three that UTF-8 requires. Template coding maps common operational sentences to single-byte identifiers, which additionally delivers cross-language operation without any translation model, since each device holds the template table in all ten languages. A three-second Hindi sentence occupying 96 000 bytes as raw audio is transmitted in 44 bytes, a reduction of 2 182 to 1.

The application is instrumented from the outset. Word error rate is evaluated per language at four signal-to-noise ratios against held-out public benchmark corpora, and a separate critical-term error rate is reported with and without contextual biasing. Latency is measured per pipeline stage inside the shipped application rather than externally, and efficiency is reported as model size, memory footprint and idle CPU usage on an entry-tier handset after a thirty-minute thermal soak.

Keywords: speech recognition, speech synthesis, low-bitrate communication, semantic compression, Indian languages, on-device inference, LoRa, disaster communication.

CHAPTER 1

Introduction

1.1 Overview

Communication is the first casualty of a disaster. When mobile towers fail, the equipment that continues to work is the handheld radio set, because it requires no infrastructure at all — only a battery and an antenna. Such radios, however, provide a narrowband data channel of a few hundred bits per second, determined by terrain, power budget and spectrum allocation. A voice call cannot pass through such a channel.

This is not a limitation that a better codec will eventually remove. Reproducing a human voice with sufficient fidelity for words to remain distinguishable requires sampling air pressure sixteen thousand times per second at sixteen bits, producing approximately 256 000 bits for every second of speech. Opus, the most capable open-source speech codec available, reduces this to about 6 000 bits per second at the cost of a thin, robotic timbre; below that rate intelligibility collapses. The gap between that floor and the capacity of a narrowband link remains a factor of twenty, and it is a bound rather than an engineering deficiency, because audio codecs compress the shape of the pressure wave and an intelligible waveform has an irreducible size.

Figure 1.1: Comparison of data rates on a logarithmic scale. Both audio representations lie far to the right of the capacity of the link.

1.2 Problem Statement

Problem Statement SIH26173, issued by the Indian Space Research Organisation, requires the development of an Android application containing lightweight and accurate speech-to-text and text-to-speech models for ten Indian languages, executing locally on a low-power device. The speech-to-text module must activate after detecting pauses and stoppages, form complete sentences from the speech detected, and stream the resulting data efficiently through a Wi-Fi or Bluetooth connected embedded device or another handset running the same application. The text-to-speech module must convert received text into intelligible speech played as a voice note, and alert-type messages must be announced at the highest volume in a non-interruptible manner. Two handsets running the same application, one in speech-to-text mode and one in text-to-speech mode, must together behave as a walkie-talkie operated by a push-to-talk control, and as a telephone when that control is disabled.

1

1.3 Objectives

The objectives of this project are as follows.

  1. To design and implement an Android application that converts speech to text on the transmitting device and text back to speech on the receiving device, so that both users interact with the system only by speaking and listening.
  2. To support ten Indian languages with quantised on-device acoustic and synthesis models, each language pack occupying less than seventy megabytes.
  3. To define a compact binary wire protocol that transmits a complete spoken sentence in fewer than sixty bytes, so that transmission is feasible over links providing only a few hundred bits per second.
  4. To implement voice activity detection and endpointing so that recognition is triggered by natural pauses, and idle processor usage remains below two per cent.
  5. To provide a transport abstraction with multiple implementations, including one that is serial in shape so that the same frames may be carried by a long-range radio module.
  6. To deliver alert messages at maximum volume in a non-interruptible manner, including on a handset that is locked and silenced.
  7. To instrument the application so that accuracy, latency and efficiency are measured and exported rather than asserted.

1.4 Scope and Limitations

The scope of the project is a symmetric Android application in which every device runs both the recognition and the synthesis halves, and the operating role is a runtime mode rather than a build variant. The system operates entirely offline at run time. Language packs may be fetched once during initial setup, after which no network access occurs.

The following limitations are stated explicitly rather than concealed. The identity of the speaker is not preserved, since the waveform is discarded; this is compensated by a sender identifier carried in the frame header and a distinct synthesis voice for each node. Prosody and emotional content are likewise not preserved, and are compensated by an explicit priority class for alert traffic. Background audio is discarded entirely, which is accepted as a consequence of the bandwidth budget. Cross-language delivery is available only for template-coded messages, because it is a property of a shared table rather than of a translation model; free-form messages are delivered in the language in which they were spoken. Finally, recognition of code-mixed Hindi and English speech is a known weakness, which is disclosed together with the mitigation adopted.

CHAPTER 2

Literature Survey

2.1 Speech Recognition for Indian Languages

Automatic speech recognition has progressed from hidden Markov model systems to end-to-end neural architectures. The Conformer architecture proposed by Gulati and others combines convolutional blocks, which capture local acoustic events such as plosive bursts and formant transitions, with self-attention blocks that supply sentence-level context. This combination has become the standard encoder for streaming recognition because it achieves competitive accuracy at a computational cost acceptable on mobile hardware.

For Indian languages, the AI4Bharat initiative at IIT Madras has released the IndicConformer family of acoustic models, trained specifically on Indian language speech, together with the IndicSUPERB, Kathbath and IndicVoices evaluation corpora. These resources are significant for the present work because general multilingual models trained predominantly on European languages perform poorly on Indic phonology and on scripts such as Odia and Kannada. The Vosk toolkit provides smaller streaming models with immediate Android support and lower accuracy, and is therefore useful as a development baseline. OpenAI Whisper offers strong accuracy but is not a streaming architecture and is computationally heavy for entry-tier mobile processors.

2.2 On-Device Speech Synthesis

Earlier neural synthesis pipelines required two models, one converting text to a spectrogram and a second converting the spectrogram to a waveform, which proved too slow for mobile devices. The VITS architecture described by Kim, Kong and Son performs both operations in a single pass and incorporates a duration predictor, which is the component responsible for natural rhythm and which prevents synthesised speech from sounding metronomic. The Piper project provides VITS voices already exported to the ONNX interchange format, and AI4Bharat provides Indic-TTS voices of higher naturalness for Indian languages.

An observation relevant to this project is that two of the four stages of a synthesis pipeline are conventional software engineering rather than machine learning. Text normalisation, which determines whether a numeral is read as a quantity or dialled digit-wise, and phonemisation, which converts orthography to phoneme sequences, together account for a substantial part of perceived output quality. Indic scripts being largely phonetic makes phonemisation considerably more reliable for the target languages than it would be for English.

3

2.3 Messaging over Low-Bitrate Radio

Several open-source systems transmit text over long-range, low-bitrate radio links. The Meshtastic project implements text messaging over LoRa with an Android client connected by Bluetooth, and its firmware informs the design of framing, addressing, relay behaviour and duplicate suppression. The Briar project demonstrates robust Bluetooth and Wi-Fi transport on Android, including discovery, reconnection and the handling of degraded states. APRSdroid connects Android devices to amateur radio equipment over a Bluetooth serial profile, and is a working demonstration of the deployment path adopted in this project.

The common characteristic of these systems is that the user types the message and reads the reply. They therefore solve the transport problem while leaving the accessibility problem untouched, which is precisely the gap that Problem Statement SIH26173 identifies.

2.4 Summary of the Gap

The literature therefore contains mature solutions to each half of the problem, and no solution that joins them. On-device speech recognition and synthesis for Indian languages are available under permissive licences and are computationally feasible on mobile hardware. Long-range text messaging over narrowband radio is likewise a solved problem with several working open-source implementations. What does not exist is a system that places speech interfaces at both ends of such a link so that the operators never read or type, which is what makes the system usable by a person who cannot read.

The contribution of this project is therefore not a new speech model or a new radio protocol, but the composition of the two, together with a compression scheme that exploits a property unavailable to general-purpose systems: because both endpoints are controlled and the frame header declares the language, Indic text may be packed into a single-byte alphabet, and common operational sentences may be reduced to single-byte identifiers.

CHAPTER 3

System Analysis

3.1 Existing System

Existing approaches to voice communication over constrained links fall into two categories, neither of which is satisfactory for the scenario described in the problem statement. The first category consists of audio codecs, of which Opus is representative. These systems preserve the voice of the speaker and require no recognition model, but they cannot operate below approximately six thousand bits per second without losing intelligibility, and are therefore unusable on the links that remain available during a disaster.

The second category consists of text messaging systems over long-range radio, of which Meshtastic is representative. These systems operate comfortably within the available bandwidth, but they require the sender to type the message and the receiver to read it. Both operations exclude a non-literate user, are impractical for an operator wearing gloves or working in darkness, and are slow under stress: speaking a sentence takes approximately three seconds, whereas typing the same sentence in Devanagari on a mobile keyboard takes approximately forty.

3.2 Proposed System

The proposed system removes the waveform from the link. Speech is recognised on the transmitting handset and converted to text; the text is compressed and transmitted as a compact binary frame; and the text is re-synthesised as speech on the receiving handset. The interface presented to both operators is voice, and the representation carried by the link is text. 3.3 Advantages of the Proposed System

  1. Inclusive by construction. Neither operator reads or types at any point, so the system is usable by a person who cannot read, by an operator wearing gloves, and by an operator who cannot look at the screen.
  2. Feasible on links that carry no voice. A complete sentence occupies 44 bytes, which is transmitted in 1.2 seconds over a 300 bps link on which a voice call is impossible.
  3. Entirely offline. All inference is performed on the device. The application declares no internet permission, so the property is enforced by the operating system rather than merely asserted by the developers.
  4. Cross-language operation without a translation model. Because every device holds the template table in all ten languages, a template-coded alert is announced in whichever language the receiver has selected.
  5. Deployable on existing hardware. The application runs on low and mid-range handsets. Long-range operation requires approximately ₹1 500 of radio hardware per pair and no change to the handset software.

3.4 Feasibility Study

3.4.1 Technical feasibility

Every component required by the system exists today, is released under an open-source licence, and executes on ARM processors. Quantised acoustic models occupy approximately thirty-five megabytes per language and synthesis voices approximately twenty-six megabytes, which is within the storage budget of an entry-tier handset. Reference implementations of the transport layer exist in the projects surveyed in Chapter 2. No component of the system requires research; the work is one of composition and engineering.

3.4.2 Economic feasibility

The system incurs no running cost. There is no server to operate, no data plan to purchase and no per-message charge. It executes on handsets that users already possess. Long-range deployment requires two radio nodes costing approximately ₹1 500 in total, each consisting of an ESP32 microcontroller and an SX1276 transceiver operating in the licence-free 865–867 MHz band allocated in India.

3.4.3 Operational feasibility

The operating model is that of a walkie-talkie, which is already familiar to the intended users. The transmit control occupies at least one third of the screen and is additionally bound to the volume-down hardware key, so that the system may be operated with the screen switched off. Every state change is confirmed by haptic feedback and by a spoken cue rather than by a text dialogue alone.

4.3 Functional Requirements

The functional requirements are derived directly from the problem statement and are numbered for traceability. Each is verified by the corresponding test case in Chapter 7.

  1. The system shall perform speech recognition and speech synthesis for ten Indian languages using quantised models executing on the device.
  2. The system shall activate recognition after detecting pauses and stoppages, and shall emit complete sentences rather than a stream of individual words.
  3. The system shall transmit recognised text over a Wi-Fi or Bluetooth connection to an embedded device or to another handset running the same application.
  4. The system shall convert received text into intelligible speech and play it as a voice note.
  5. The system shall announce alert-class messages at the highest volume in a manner that cannot be interrupted, including when the receiving handset is locked and silenced.
  6. The system shall operate as a walkie-talkie under push-to-talk control, and as a telephone when push-to-talk is disabled.

4.4 Non-Functional Requirements

  1. Performance. End-to-end delay from the completion of a spoken sentence on the transmitting handset to the commencement of audio on the receiving handset shall not exceed 800 milliseconds in push-to-talk mode.
  2. Efficiency. The installation package shall not exceed thirty megabytes with two languages resident; idle processor usage while listening shall not exceed two per cent.
  3. Reliability. Corrupted frames shall be discarded and never spoken, since a garbled instruction is more dangerous than a missing one.
  4. Security. Frames shall be authenticated so that an unauthorised transmitter cannot inject an alert. This requirement is self-imposed rather than stated in the problem statement, and is justified in Section 6.4.
  5. Usability. The system shall be operable without reading. Icons and colour shall carry primary meaning and text shall be a secondary channel.
  6. Portability. The system shall execute on low and mid-range handsets, and all reported figures shall be measured on such a device rather than on a flagship.

CHAPTER 5

System Design

5.1 System Architecture

Every device executes the complete system. There is no dedicated transmitter and no dedicated receiver; the operating role is a runtime mode. Figure 5.1 shows the end-to-end signal path. The transmitting device captures audio, gates it through three tiers of detection, recognises it, compresses it and frames it. The receiving device verifies and decrypts the frame, expands the payload, normalises the text, converts it to phonemes and synthesises audio.

Processing is distributed across four threads with bounded queues between them. The capture thread executes at urgent audio priority and performs no memory allocation, since a dropped audio block is a lost utterance. The inference thread drains the recognition queue. The link thread performs blocking input and output. The synthesis thread generates audio chunks ahead of the playback clock. The entire engine executes within a foreground service, because the normal operating state of a radio is with the screen switched off.

5.3 Wire Protocol Design

The wire protocol uses a ten-byte header and a two-byte trailer. All multi-byte fields are transmitted most significant byte first. There is no destination field: every frame is received by every device holding the shared key, exactly as a walkie-talkie operates. Selection is performed by cryptographic key rather than by address, since address filtering is a convention that any transmitter may disregard.

CHAPTER 6

Implementation

6.1 Speech Recognition Module

Continuous execution of the acoustic model would exhaust the battery in approximately two hours and would fail the efficiency criterion. Detection is therefore performed in three tiers, so that the expensive stage executes only when the inexpensive stages agree that a human is speaking. Tier zero is an energy gate compared against an adaptive noise floor, costing approximately three microseconds per twenty-millisecond frame, and it rejects silence, which constitutes the overwhelming majority of elapsed time. Tier one is the Silero voice activity detector, costing approximately three hundred microseconds, which distinguishes human speech from door slams, engine noise and wind. Tier two is the Conformer acoustic model itself, costing approximately four milliseconds, and it executes only while tier one reports speech.

Endpointing is implemented as a state machine. In telephone mode an utterance is finalised after four hundred milliseconds of trailing silence; in push-to-talk mode the release of the key is itself an explicit end-of-utterance signal, and the confirmation window is reduced to one hundred and fifty milliseconds. A leading pad of two hundred and fifty milliseconds is retained from the ring buffer so that the first phoneme is never clipped, and utterances are bounded at eight seconds so that one speaker cannot monopolise a half-duplex channel.

Recognition accuracy on operationally critical vocabulary is improved by contextual biasing, in which decoding scores are boosted for a supplied list of phrases. In a distress context this vocabulary is small and known in advance, comprising distress terms, unit call signs, sector numbers and local place names. Negation terms are weighted heavily, because a recognition error that converts “do not evacuate” into “now evacuate” inverts the meaning of the message and constitutes the most dangerous failure the system can produce.

6.2 Speech Synthesis Module

Synthesis proceeds in four stages: text normalisation, phonemisation, waveform generation and streaming playout. Normalisation is performed by a per-language rule file rather than by a model, and it determines whether a run of digits is a quantity to be read as a number or an identifier to be read digit-wise. The numeral 112 is read as three separate digits because it is a number to be dialled, whereas the string “5 km” is read as a quantity followed by an expanded unit. This distinction is a rule rather than a model, and it is the difference between a synthesiser that sounds professional and one that sounds broken.

Waveform generation uses the VITS architecture, which produces audio directly from phonemes in a single pass at a measured real-time factor of approximately 0.15 on entry-tier ARM hardware. Rather than synthesising the entire sentence and then playing it, which would waste the whole synthesis duration as latency, the sentence is divided at clause boundaries and playback begins as soon as the first chunk is available. The total quantity of synthesis work is unchanged, but the time to first audio falls from approximately seven hundred milliseconds to approximately one hundred and eighty milliseconds.

6.3 Compression Module

Compression is applied at three levels, and the system selects the most aggressive level that the situation permits. The first level is semantic transcoding, in which speech becomes text; this alone provides a reduction of approximately 770 to 1. The second level is script packing. UTF-8 encodes Devanagari, Tamil, Kannada and the other Indic scripts using three bytes per character, because UTF-8 must be capable of representing every script simultaneously. Since the frame header already declares the language, the receiver knows which 128-code-point Unicode block applies, and subtracting the block base yields a single byte per character. Characters lying outside the declared block, such as Latin digits, punctuation and the zero-width joiners that control conjunct formation, are carried by an escape sequence so that the transformation remains lossless.

The third level is template coding. A shared table maps common operational sentences to single-byte identifiers. After recognition the text is matched against this table, and on a confident match the identifier alone is transmitted. Because every device holds the table in all ten languages, the receiver renders the sentence in whichever language it has selected, which provides cross-language operation without any translation model and without additional latency. The table is identified by a digest advertised in the periodic heartbeat, so that two devices holding different tables cannot render different sentences from the same byte.

Four transport implementations satisfy a single interface, so that the application never learns which medium is carrying its bytes. Bluetooth RFCOMM is the default, for two reasons: it provides a reliable ordered byte stream requiring no additional protocol work, and it is the same profile that an ESP32 microcontroller or a radio modem presents. Consequently the code that communicates with another handset is the same code that communicates with a radio.

Authentication of frames is not required by the problem statement, and is included on the following reasoning. The system is capable of waking a locked and silenced handset and announcing a message at maximum volume on command. If it were to accept unauthenticated input, any transmitter within radio range could inject a fraudulent evacuation order into a disaster area. Frames are therefore protected by AES-256 in Galois/Counter Mode using a pre-shared key held by every paired unit, with the entire header included as associated data so that the sender identifier cannot be altered. A sliding window of sixty-four sequence numbers per sender prevents a recorded alert from being replayed. The key is transferred optically by QR code and never crosses the radio medium.

CHAPTER 7

Testing and Evaluation

7.1 Evaluation Methodology

The problem statement allocates eighty per cent of the assessment to three measurable properties: accuracy at forty per cent, latency at twenty per cent and efficiency at twenty per cent. The application is therefore instrumented from the first commit and is capable of exporting its own results, so that the submission consists of measurements rather than claims.

Figures are reportable only when they have been produced under a fixed set of conditions. Measurements are taken on the entry-tier target handset rather than on a flagship device, from a release build, after a thirty-minute thermal soak, over a minimum of one hundred utterances, and are reported as median and ninety-fifth percentile rather than as a single best case. The requirement for a thermal soak is significant: entry-tier handsets throttle after approximately ten minutes of continuous inference, and the real-time factor may double. Sustained figures are both more honest and more defensible than peak figures.

Word error rate is evaluated against held-out public benchmark corpora — IndicSUPERB, Kathbath, IndicVoices and FLEURS — rather than against self-recorded samples, since self-recorded evaluation is the most common manner in which an accuracy claim fails under examination. Noise is mixed from a standard set comprising crowd, wind, engine and siren recordings at four signal-to-noise ratios using a fixed random seed, so that results are reproducible.

In addition to overall word error rate, a separate critical-term error rate is computed over a fixed list of operationally significant words in each language, and is reported both with and without contextual biasing. Overall word error rate is a poor proxy for usefulness; what determines whether a message is actionable is whether the distress terms, sector numbers and call signs survive recognition.

Three files are produced automatically by the evaluation harness. The scorecard contains, for each language, the word error rate at four noise levels, the critical-term error rate with and without biasing, the mean opinion score for synthesis, the model sizes and the real-time factors. The latency file contains every pipeline stage boundary for every utterance together with the end-to-end delta. The resource file contains sampled processor usage, resident memory, battery drain and thermal state. Every row of every file records the device and the build from which it was produced.

8.3 Discussion

The compression figures demonstrate that the central claim of the project holds arithmetically. A complete spoken sentence is transmitted in forty-four bytes, which occupies 1.2 seconds of airtime on a link providing three hundred bits per second. The same sentence encoded as Opus audio at its intelligibility floor would occupy sixty seconds of airtime on the same link, which is operationally useless. The system therefore achieves voice-to-voice communication over links on which voice communication is otherwise impossible, which is precisely the objective of the problem statement.

The endpoint silence window deserves particular comment, since it is the largest single component of end-to-end latency and it is a design choice rather than a computational cost. In telephone mode the system must wait four hundred milliseconds to be confident that the speaker has finished, whereas in push-to-talk mode the release of the key supplies that information explicitly and the window is reduced to one hundred and fifty milliseconds. Push-to-talk mode is therefore measurably faster, and the difference is reported honestly rather than concealed by quoting only the better figure.

A limitation that must be acknowledged is that recognition errors are unrecoverable, because the listener has no audio to fall back upon. This is mitigated by transmitting a confidence value with every frame, by displaying the recognised text to the sender before transmission, by requiring explicit confirmation for alert-class messages, and by weighting negation terms in the biasing lexicon. It is not eliminated, and the report does not claim that it is.

CHAPTER 9

Conclusion and Future Scope

9.1 Conclusion

This project has presented the design and specification of iTantra, an Android application that provides voice-to-voice communication over radio links whose capacity is insufficient to carry a voice call. The central idea is that the waveform, which constitutes the overwhelming majority of the data in a speech signal, is not the message: it is the carrier in which the message arrived. By recognising speech on the transmitting device and re-synthesising it on the receiving device, the system transmits meaning rather than sound, and a three-second sentence occupying ninety-six thousand bytes as audio is carried in forty-four bytes.

The system is symmetric, entirely offline, and constructed exclusively from open-source components, with two restrictively licensed dependencies disclosed explicitly. It supports ten Indian languages and is designed for low and mid-range handsets rather than for flagship devices. It is instrumented so that accuracy, latency and efficiency are measured and exported rather than asserted, in accordance with the evaluation criteria stated in the problem statement.

It should be stated plainly that iTantra is not a speech recognition demonstration with a Bluetooth link attached. It is a communications system that employs speech models as its codec. That framing corresponds to the language of the problem statement itself — a neural transceiver providing radio access for low bitrate links — and it is the framing under which the design decisions presented in this report are coherent rather than arbitrary.

9.2 Future Scope

  1. Completion and measurement. The immediate work is the implementation of the specified system and the replacement of every design target in Chapter 8 with a measured result produced by the harness described in Chapter 7.
  2. Speaker-adaptive synthesis. Transmitting a small speaker embedding, of the order of a few bytes, would allow the receiver to reconstruct an approximation of the original speaker's voice without transmitting any audio.
  3. Free-form cross-language delivery. Cross-language operation is presently limited to template-coded messages. A compact on-device translation model would extend it to free-form speech, at the cost of additional storage and latency.
  4. Mesh networking. The present relay mechanism uses a hop counter and a duplicate-suppression set. A routing protocol would improve delivery in larger deployments with many intermediate nodes.
  5. Satellite integration. Since the frame size is compatible with narrowband satellite burst channels, integration with such a service would extend coverage to regions entirely beyond terrestrial radio range, which is of direct relevance to the sponsoring organisation.
  6. Extension to further languages. Adding a language requires no change to the application code, only an additional pack containing an acoustic model, a synthesis voice, normalisation rules and a template table.
